From this toy to real training.

The curve is a map of one part of training—not a tiny replica of the whole thing. These are the exact ideas it stands in for, plus the pieces we have intentionally left outside the frame.

Puck position → parameters

The model’s learnable weights, compressed here to one coordinate on the horizontal axis.

L
Curve height → loss

A score for how wrong the current model is. Lower points on the curve mean lower loss.

∇
Arrow → gradient

Backpropagation computes which weight changes would raise loss fastest; we go opposite.

↘

The optimizer repeatedly changes weights. One breadcrumb is one training step.

⌁

Choices made by the trainer—not learned weights. They shape how learning behaves.

What this toy hides

No rug pulls later.

The metaphor is useful because we keep its boundary visible.

  • Real landscapes have millions or billions of dimensions, not one—and we cannot see the whole surface.
  • A batch only estimates the gradient. Its sampling noise is more structured than our “shake” knob.
  • Low training loss is not the finish line. Validation data tells us whether the model generalizes.
  • Hyperparameter tuning compares full runs, often changing schedules, batch size, regularization, and optimizer—not just three knobs.

Field guide · Axolotl

Where the toy becomes a training config.

The visualization uses normalized units. Axolotl uses literal optimizer values, batches, schedules, and regularization. Treat this as a vocabulary map and starting experiment—not a universal recipe.

Axolotl YAML

reference, not prescription 
# optimizer and run shape
adapter: qlora
optimizer: paged_adamw_32bit
learning_rate: 2.0e-4
lr_scheduler: cosine
warmup_ratio: 0.10
weight_decay: 0.0

# effective batch = 2 × 4 × GPU count
micro_batch_size: 2
gradient_accumulation_steps: 4
num_epochs: 4
max_grad_norm: 1.0
Full fine-tune usually starts with a smaller learning rate. LoRA / QLoRA often tolerates a larger adapter learning rate. Validation loss decides whether the configuration generalizes.
